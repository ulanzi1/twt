# Why this addendum

The note we sent offers a list of **adults only**, because that is what you ruled in October for the family's words: the family is asked
⛔ not to name children, and the reviewer leaves out any child's name. On reflection, BigDev asks whether that is right. Indian obituaries
routinely name children and grandchildren; the person sending is very often the children's parent or guardian; and the Trust can check
⛔ neither a person's age nor who their guardian is — so the reviewer's "leave out any child's name" can only ever catch the names that
*look* like children's. The note as sent gave you ⛔ no option to allow it.

# AQ1 — On the family list, may a child be named if the person sending declares they are the child's guardian?

**In one sentence:** before sending, the nominee confirms — once, for the whole list — that for every child named they are the child's
parent or lawful guardian or have that person's agreement, and that every adult named has agreed to be named; the Trust relies on that
and does ⛔ not leave out a child's name the declaration covers.

**Why it cannot be decided without you:** it decides whether a child's name appears on a page anyone can open, kept for good, on the
strength of a family member's word.

### The one fact that decides it

The Trust **cannot verify** who is a child or who their guardian is — under any option. ⇒ The real choice is ⛔ not "checked" against
"unchecked"; it is whether responsibility for a child's name sits with **the family, by a declaration they sign**, or with **the Trust,
by a blanket rule that leaves out every child's name it can spot**.

| | Option | What changes for a person | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** ⭐ | **A declaration.** At Send the nominee confirms: *"For every child I have named, I am their parent or lawful guardian, or I have their parent's or guardian's agreement. Every adult I have named has agreed to be named."* The list may then include children by name; the reviewer does ⛔ not leave out a name the declaration covers, and still applies the other three reasons | A family can write its obituary the way families do — *पौत्र · आरव* — and the adults named have been asked first | The Trust relies on a statement it cannot check: if it is false (the sender is ⛔ not the guardian, or never asked an adult), a child's or an adult's name is up on an open page, kept for good, until someone tells the Trust. A child cannot agree for themselves, and cannot later remove their own name unless you choose the removal route in the main note (PQ2 B or C). One more step for a grieving family, and some will tick it without asking anyone. Counsel has ⛔ not been asked whether a declaration like this is enough consent for a child in law |
| **B** | **Adults only**, as in the note as sent: the family is asked ⛔ not to list children, and the reviewer leaves out any child's name it spots | ⛔ No child's name on the list | Families cannot write the obituary they expect; and since the reviewer cannot tell a person's age, the rule protects only the children whose names it recognises — the rest stay up regardless |
| **C** | **Children by relation only**: adults by name; children counted, ⛔ not named (*"and three grandchildren"*) | The family's shape is shown; ⛔ no child is named | Still less than families expect; the reviewer still has to decide who is a child |

**Our reading:** **A** — **because** the Trust cannot tell who is a child under any option, so a blanket rule protects less than it
appears to; a declaration puts the responsibility with the person who actually knows, asks the adults named for their agreement (which
today nobody does), and keeps the Trust's other three reasons in force.

# AQ2 — Should the same declaration apply to the family's own words?

**In one sentence:** in October you ruled that the family is asked ⛔ not to name children in its words and the reviewer leaves out any
child's name. Should the declaration in AQ1 apply there too?

| | Option | What changes for a person | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** ⭐ | **Yes — one rule for the words and the list.** A child's name the declaration covers stays in the words as written. This **changes your October ruling** for the words, and "it names a child" stays one of the four reasons only for a child the declaration does ⛔ not cover | The same family can name its grandchildren in the words and in the list | Every cost of AQ1 A, now for the words too; it reverses a ruling you made eight days ago |
| **B** | **No — the October ruling stands for the words** | Children's names are still left out of the words | A family could name a grandchild in the list but ⛔ not in its own words beside it — a page that reads as inconsistent, and a rule families will find hard to follow |

**Our reading:** **A**, **if** you choose AQ1 A — **because** one page should follow one rule. If you choose AQ1 B or C, AQ2 falls away.

# AQ3 — Should the same declaration cover a photo that shows a child?

**In one sentence:** in October you ruled that the reviewer leaves out the family's photo if it shows a child. Should a declaration that
the sender is the guardian of every child in the photo (or has the guardian's agreement) let the photo stay?

**Why it cannot be decided without you:** it decides whether a child's **face** appears on a page anyone can open, kept for good — and it
changes your October ruling.

### The one fact that decides it

A family photo very often shows grandchildren with the member — it is the photo a family most wants to share. But a face identifies a
child more surely, and for longer, than a name: it can be copied and matched, and the reviewer cannot leave a face out of a photo the way
it can leave a name out of a sentence — it is the whole photo or nothing.

| | Option | What changes for a person | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** ⭐ | **Yes — one rule for the words, the list and the photo.** With the declaration, a photo showing a child stays; the declaration's words add *"… and every child in the photo"*. "It shows a child" stays a reason only for a child the declaration does ⛔ not cover | A family can share the photo it most wants — the member with their grandchildren | A child's face on an open page, kept for good, on a family member's word. A photo is ⛔ not proof of guardianship — a neighbour, teacher or relative may hold the same photo — so it does ⛔ not make the declaration more reliable. Under PQ2 B / C a named person may ask for their **name** off; ⛔ nothing yet lets a person ask for a photo of themselves to come down except the nominee's take-down of the whole memorial. Counsel ⛔ not asked |
| **B** | **No — the October rule stands for photos**: a photo showing a child is left out, declaration or not (names may still follow AQ1 / AQ2) | ⛔ No child's face on the page | The family cannot share a photo with the grandchildren in it; and a page could name a grandchild but ⛔ not show the photo beside it |

**Our reading:** **A**, **if** you choose AQ1 A — **because** a family that may name its grandchildren will expect to show the photo
of them with the member, and one declaration for one page is the rule families can follow. ⚠ The drafter's own view was B, for the
face-versus-name reason in the one fact; BigDev's reading is A. Both are put so you can weigh them.

### AQ3 (b) — if A: an optional "who is in the photo"

Under AQ3 A, the family may — if it wishes — tick which people **already on its family list** appear in the photo. ⛔ No new names are
asked for: a tick only points at a listed person. The ticks are used **by the Trust's reviewer and for the declaration** — they show the
reviewer exactly which relations are in the photo (a ticked *grandson* tells the reviewer what the declaration is vouching for, where
today the reviewer can only guess from a face) — and, later, they would let one person's photo be found if that person asks to come
off. The declaration still covers every child in the photo, ticked or ⛔ not. The family's own photo description, in its own words,
is unchanged and follows AQ2.

The question for you is only whether the ticks are also **shown on the public page**, as a caption under the photo:

| | Option | What changes for a person | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(b)1** ⭐ | **Not shown.** The ticks stay with the review and the declaration | ⛔ No name is placed next to a face by the Trust; the family may still describe the photo in its own words | The page does ⛔ not say who is who unless the family writes it; the Trust holds one more fact about each listed person (whether they are in the photo) — kept for good, with the list |
| **(b)2** | **Shown as a caption** — *"In the photo: wife — Sunita Devi; grandson — Aarav"* | Readers know who is in the photo | A child's name next to their face on an open page — the most identifying pairing there is — kept for good; anyone who copies the photo copies the caption's link to a name |

**Our reading:** **(b)1** — **because** the ticks earn their place by making the declaration and the review exact; showing them adds
exposure for little gain, and a family that wants a caption can write one in its own description.

# In plain English

The Trust cannot tell who is a child, or who is a child's guardian — so a rule that "the Trust leaves out every child's name" only ever
catches the names that look like children's. BigDev suggests instead that the person sending simply declares that they are the parent or
guardian of any child they name (or have that person's agreement), and that the adults they name have agreed. The family then writes its
obituary the way families do, the adults named are asked first, and responsibility for each name sits with the person who knows. The
risk is a false declaration — a name up on an open page that should ⛔ not be — and the only remedy for that is letting a named person ask
for their own name off (the main note's PQ2). The same question arises for a photo showing a child (AQ3): BigDev suggests one rule for all three; a face is more lasting than a name, which is why it is asked separately. The family may also tick who on its list is in the photo, to make the declaration and the review exact; we suggest the ticks are ⛔ not shown as a caption (AQ3 (b)). We suggest A to all three, with counsel asked about the law.
